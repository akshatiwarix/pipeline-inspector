# Pipeline Inspector, in plain English

You don't need to read any code to understand what this is or why it exists. This is for anyone who has ever looked at a sales pipeline and had a bad feeling about a deal they couldn't quite put into words.

## The problem, in three parts

**"Stalled" usually just means someone's gut feeling.** Ask two sales managers to look at the same pipeline report and point out the deals that are "stuck," and you'll get two different answers. Neither one is wrong, exactly — but neither one is applying the same rule either. There's no shared definition of "stuck," so the label is really just a feeling dressed up as an observation.

**"Risky" and "stalled" aren't the same problem, but people talk about them like they are.** A deal can be moving through the pipeline exactly on schedule and still be quietly dangerous — maybe only one person at the buying company has ever talked to you, or the close date keeps getting pushed back a little at a time. Meanwhile, a totally different deal might be "stuck" in the same stage for weeks even though everyone's still emailing back and forth constantly — busy, but not actually progressing. If you call both of these "at risk," you've told a rep nothing about what to actually do differently.

**Nobody ever checks whether the warning flags were right.** Plenty of tools will confidently tell you "this deal looks risky." Almost none of them ever go back and check: of all the deals we flagged, how many of them actually turned out badly? Without that check, a risk flag is just a guess wearing a badge.

## What this tool does about it

Pipeline Inspector looks at a set of open deals — deals that are still in progress, not deals that have already closed — and runs five separate, very specific checks against each one:

1. **Has anyone touched this deal recently?** If it's been quiet for two weeks or more, that's flagged.
2. **Has this deal been sitting in the same stage too long?** Each stage has its own reasonable time limit — a deal shouldn't linger in early discovery as long as it might linger in final negotiation.
3. **Is only one person involved?** If a whole deal rests on a relationship with a single contact, that's fragile — if that person leaves or goes quiet, the deal has nobody left to talk to.
4. **Has the expected close date been pushed back more than once?** One delay happens. Two or more starts to look like a pattern.
5. **Is this a big, late-stage deal that's gone quiet?** A $150,000 deal that's been silent for a week in the final negotiation stage deserves more urgency than a $20,000 deal that's just starting out.

Each of these checks is kept completely separate from the others. A deal can trip one, several, or none of them, and the tool always shows you *which* one and *why* — pointing to the exact emails, calls, or meetings that led to that conclusion. Nothing is a black-box score.

## How we know the flags actually mean something

Behind the scenes, every one of the 50 example deals in this tool was secretly built to be either genuinely healthy, genuinely stalling, or genuinely at risk — a hidden answer key, written in before any of the checking rules ever ran. The five checks above never get to see that hidden answer. They only ever look at the same information a real sales manager would have: activity history, contacts, dates, dollar amounts.

Afterward, we compare the two. If the checks are doing their job, deals secretly built to be healthy should almost never get flagged, and deals secretly built to be stalling or at-risk should get flagged the vast majority of the time. On this tool's example data, that's exactly what happens: only about 1 in 10 healthy deals gets flagged, while every single stalling or at-risk deal does. That comparison is the whole point — it's the difference between a tool that *asserts* it's useful and one that can actually show its work.

## What this isn't

This isn't connected to a real CRM, and it isn't reading anyone's actual sales data — every deal in it is invented, generated by a small, published recipe so that anyone can check exactly how it was built. There's no AI model making judgment calls here either; every single flag comes from a plain, fixed rule that you could just as easily check by hand with a calculator and a calendar. That's deliberate — the point of this project is to prove that useful, evidence-backed pipeline flags don't require anything more exotic than a clear definition of what "stalled" and "risky" actually mean.
